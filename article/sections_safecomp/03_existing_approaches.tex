\section{Existing Coverage and Analysis Approaches}
\label{chapter:existing_approaches}

Foundational terminology for automated driving is established by the SAE J3016 taxonomy \cite{ORAD2021taxonomy}, which defines six levels of driving automation and key concepts such as the Dynamic Driving Task (DDT) 
and Operational Design Domain (ODD). Building on this, \cite{deGelder2022ontology} propose an object-oriented framework for scenario definition that ensures the scenarios constructed are complete and comparable.

Scenario-based testing has emerged as the dominant paradigm for validating automated driving functions. The PEGASUS project \cite{pegasus2019method} introduces a six-layer model to decompose the driving environment into structured logical scenarios, 
shifting validation from public road testing to scenario exploration across simulation and field tests. \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1801-08598} refine this with three abstraction levels---functional, logical, 
and concrete scenarios---while noting that existing parameter-selection methods lack a systematic way to determine meaningful test coverage. Motivated by this weak point, \cite{Ries2021traj_clustering} present trajectory-based clustering of real-world driving data to structure the scenario space and reduce test-set redundancy. Complementing such data-driven structuring, \cite{zhang2026adversarial} generate adversarial test scenes that explicitly target harmful, rare, and ambiguous situations.

A central challenge is the \emph{approval trap} identified by \cite{wachenfeld2016release}: statistically demonstrating superior safety through real-world driving would require billions of test kilometres, motivating simulation-based tools and systematic coverage methods. \cite{feng2023dense} tackle this bottleneck directly with a dense reinforcement-learning environment that accelerates safety validation by orders of magnitude. Domain-specific coverage buckets and performance metrics are proposed in \cite{foretellix2019}, while ISO~21448 \cite{iso21448} and UL~4600 \cite{ul4600} standardize coverage criteria for automated driving.

More recent work represents traffic scenes directly as graphs to enable scalable, label-free analysis of the scenario space. \cite{zipfl2022selfsupervised} encode semantic scene graphs into low-dimensional embeddings via a Siamese network to cluster traffic scenes without manual labels, an approach refined through graph-based contrastive learning in \cite{zipfl2023similarity} and extended to heterogeneous scenario graphs for clustering and similarity search by \cite{muetsch2025semantic}. Targeting scalability, \cite{mo2025sparscene} propose SparScene, a sparse graph-learning framework that exploits lane-graph topology to build structure-aware actor--map connections, scaling scene representation to thousands of actors for large-scale trajectory generation, with related work applying graph structure learning to traffic-scene representation and encoding more broadly~\cite{mo2025graphstructure}. Cluster-based analyses have likewise been used to assess the completeness of scenario categories \cite{rossberg2025completeness}, complementing the standardized coverage criteria discussed above. These graph-based representations directly motivate our framework, which combines explicit subgraph isomorphism against archetype graphs with learned GINE embeddings to quantify coverage of the scenario space.
